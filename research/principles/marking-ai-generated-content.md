# Marking AI-generated content

How an interface should tell people which content a model wrote, now that several design systems publish a pattern for it and the EU requires it.

A designer opens an audit report and reads twelve component descriptions and a list of findings. Some were written by a person, some filled in from a template, and some drafted by a model that nobody has checked yet. Nothing on the page says which is which, so the designer trusts all of it equally.

The systems that have thought about this agree that the page should say so, and so does EU law. What's left to decide is how.

## Marker wording

A short text label, such as "Generated by AI"; an icon may sit beside it, but never replaces it.

Take the audit report. Each model-drafted description carries a small sparkle in its corner, and nothing else. A designer who has learned the sparkle reads it correctly. A new teammate sees decoration, and a screen reader announces nothing at all, or "image".

The sparkle on the left means something only to people who already know it. The words on the right mean the same thing to everyone, sighted or not.

That is why the systems that publish a pattern all lead with words:

- [Cloudscape's generative AI output label](https://cloudscape.design/patterns/genai/output-label/) reads "Generated by AI".
- [SAP Fiori's local AI notice](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-145/ui-elements/local-ai-notice/usage) reads "Created with AI. Verify before use.", shortened to "Created with AI." only where space is limited. It "must never be truncated".
- [Red Hat's AI transparency notices](https://ux.redhat.com/ai-guidelines/transparency-notices/) ask for "one visual and one verbal indicator" at minimum, with wording such as "[Feature name] uses AI. Check for mistakes."
- [Carbon for AI](https://carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/) makes its AI label a component, the primary indicator that AI is present and the way into its explainability content.

The sparkle has become the common glyph anyway, and Cloudscape protects it: its sparkle label is not for other forms of AI that aren't generative. A glyph carries meaning only while nothing else uses it, so reserving it is what keeps it worth reading.

The systems differ in what the label opens. Here they are side by side.

| System | Label text | Icon | Opens an explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon | AI label component | Yes | Yes, an explainability popover, required on every AI component |
| Cloudscape | "Generated by AI" | Sparkle, generative AI only | Not specified |
| SAP Fiori | "Created with AI. Verify before use." | Not specified in the label | Interactive variant: popover, then a help page |
| Red Hat | "[Feature] uses AI. Check for mistakes." and others | Required alongside text | Persistent notice under an information icon in assistants |
| Lily | Headless inline indicator | Consumer's choice | Recommends wrapping in a button or popover |

Carbon is the only one that requires an explanation behind every label; the others leave it optional or unstated.

Write the label as text in your component, with the sparkle as an optional companion that has no accessible name of its own. **The word is the marker; the icon is decoration beside it.** If your design ever needs an icon-only marker, give it an accessible name that says the same words. The toolkit's content page states the same rule (`knowledge/ui/design/content.md#ai-disclosure`), and on this point every system read agrees.

## Label placement

The label goes once, next to the output, at the lowest heading that contains every AI result; never on each item.

The audit report has a section of findings, every one drafted by a model. A team that labels each finding ends up with a column of identical badges down the page.

On the left the badge becomes wallpaper by the third row. On the right it is read once, and it covers everything below it.

Wallpaper is the failure the systems keep naming, because it's ignored exactly where the reader most needs to notice it. [Cloudscape](https://cloudscape.design/patterns/genai/output-label/) asks for "close proximity to the output" and warns against redundant labels: when every item in a list is AI-generated, label the group header, not each item.

SAP sets the rule most precisely: one label per screen, "next to the lowest title element that contains all AI results." It also scales placement to the impact of the decision the content feeds:

- **High and very high impact.** The label goes between the section header and the content, and the interactive variant is recommended.
- **Low and medium impact.** The label may sit at the end of the content area, with an invisible region label such as "<region name>. Contains AI-generated content." for assistive technology.

For your audit report, put one label beside the *Findings* heading and none on the rows. **Label the smallest container that holds all the AI output, once.** Where the content feeds a decision that matters, move the label above the content and make it open an explanation.

## Labels after review

The label changes after review: AI provenance is a state, not a decoration, so the label has to render from data and change when a person edits or reviews the text.

A model drafts a component description. A designer reads it, fixes a wrong prop name and approves it. If the label was typed into the page by hand, it still says "Generated by AI" a month later, about text a person now stands behind.

[Carbon for AI](https://carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/) handles the edit case most clearly. When a person overrides AI-suggested content, the component switches from the AI variant to the default one. The person can still get back to the original, through a "revert to AI" button.

SAP covers the review case from the other side. Its read-only variant exists for when "no details on how AI results were validated can be provided", which implies the interactive one should say how they were.

The toolkit adds the review state to the words themselves, as its own rule for its own generated text (`knowledge/ui/design/content.md#ai-disclosure`):

| Source of the string | Review state | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Model | No recorded review | Drafted by AI, not yet reviewed |
| Model | Review recorded | Drafted by AI, reviewed by <name> |
| Template | Not applicable | None |

Template text carries no label because it isn't AI-generated, and labelling it would devalue the label. Each string's source (template, model or person) and its review state are fields in data, and the label is rendered from them, never written by hand.

Store a source field and a review field beside every generated string, and render the label from those two fields. **A label that never changes after review "has become wallpaper; wire it to the review record."** Carbon's switch on edit is published practice; the review-state wording is the toolkit's own rule, adopted because it tells the reader whether anyone has checked the text.

## Reserving the AI look

The AI look stays with AI content: if AI content gets its own visual style, that style belongs to AI content and nowhere else.

A designer building a marketing banner reaches for the soft purple glow the AI panel uses, because it looks fresh. Next week a reader sees the glow on the banner and wonders what the model wrote.

[Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/) styles AI with "light-inspired effects like brightness, glow, and gradients," a metaphor for illuminating AI-generated content. It then forbids borrowing them: "Don't use the Carbon for AI styling as decoration."

Glow and gradient are exactly the decoration a design standard otherwise refuses. The trade is defensible only while the style means one thing, and like the sparkle, it stops meaning anything once it appears elsewhere.

Keep the AI style in its own tokens, and use them only inside AI-labelled components. **The AI look is a signal, so it can't double as decoration.** The rule is checkable: search your components for the AI tokens and confirm every hit sits inside a labelled one.

## EU law now

Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires that people be told when they deal with an AI system and that generated content be machine-marked; the Digital Omnibus did not postpone it.

A product team shipping a model-drafted summary to European customers in autumn 2026 is no longer choosing whether to disclose it. The question is which duty applies, and when.

[Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) has two parts that matter here:

- People must be "informed that they are interacting with an AI system", unless it is obvious.
- Providers of systems that generate synthetic text, images, audio or video must ensure outputs "are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated".

The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force from 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk obligations: to 2 December 2027 for Annex III and to 2 August 2028 for Annex I. It left transparency in place, with one transitional period. Generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 for the marking duty. The Commission adopted guidelines on the transparency obligations on 20 July 2026, [according to Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force).

Design systems have started to cite it. SAP Fiori now refers to Article 50 directly and calls its notice mandatory for "all user interfaces that involve AI-generated or AI-edited information."

The confusing part is that the law's machine-readable marking and the visible label are different things. The marking is a property of the output file or stream. A visible label doesn't satisfy it, and it doesn't replace a visible label.

Treat the visible label as design work and the machine-readable marking as engineering work, and plan both. **A visible label is not the law's marking, and the marking is not a label.** Whether a design toolkit's generated audit text falls within Article 50 is a legal question for a lawyer, not this guide; the design answer is the same either way.

## Cheat sheet

| Decision | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Marker | Short text label, icon optional | Icon alone, or a glow alone |
| Wording | "Generated by AI", "Drafted by AI, not yet reviewed" | Vague "Smart" or "Magic" names |
| Placement | Next to the output, once, at the lowest heading containing all of it | A label on every row |
| Impact | Above the content, interactive, for high-impact decisions | The same quiet footer label for a decision that matters |
| State | Rendered from a provenance field; changes on edit and review | A hand-written label that never changes |
| Template output | No label | Labelling deterministic text as AI |
| Style | AI style only inside labelled regions | Borrowing the AI glow as decoration |
| Explanation | Label opens an explanation on request | An explanation forced on every view |

## The short version

Say it in words: "Generated by AI", with a sparkle beside it if you like, never a sparkle alone. Put one label next to the output, at the lowest heading that holds all of it, and raise it above the content when the decision matters. Render it from a source field and a review field in your data, so it changes when a person edits or reviews the text, and leave template text unlabelled. Keep any AI visual style for the label alone. In the EU this is no longer optional: Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026, and its machine-readable marking is a separate engineering job from the label. The systems agree on words, proximity and one label per region; the review-state wording is the toolkit's own rule.
